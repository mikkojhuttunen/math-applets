From Speed to Distance: the Integral as Accumulation

A car's speedometer reads v(t) in m/s. The integral of v(t) over time answers a very practical question: how far did the car travel? Drag the sliders to see how the sum of many thin rectangles (Riemann sum) converges to the exact distance — the definite integral.

Why this is an integral. Speed alone doesn't tell you distance — you need to accumulate it over time. Chopping the interval [0, T] into n thin slices of width Δt, each slice contributes roughly v(t) · Δt — the distance covered at (nearly) constant speed during that sliver. Adding up all the slivers gives the Riemann sum shown above.

As n grows, the rectangles hug the curve more tightly and the sum converges to the exact area under v(t): ∫₀ᵀ v(t) dt. That limit — the exact, error-free distance — is what the integral computes directly, without ever chopping anything up.

The same idea works for any accumulation: flow rate integrated over time gives volume filled, power integrated over time gives energy used, population growth rate integrated gives total population change. Wherever a quantity is built up from a rate, an integral is the tool that adds it all together.

Try dragging n down to 1–3 to see how rough the approximation is, then up past 80 to watch it lock onto the exact value.
